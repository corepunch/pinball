# Freeze the base, then break symmetry above

> **Historical research:** measurements, proposed mechanisms, and owner-decision notes below describe
> earlier boards. The shipped `aurora` is currently the bare shared base, with no upper deck or ramp.
> Use [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md) and [ARCHITECTURE.md](../ARCHITECTURE.md) for current contracts;
> verify dimensions against `Scenes/` before applying old ball-relative measurements.
> Local `reference/repos/` research checkouts are not bundled; their file citations below are archival paths.
> For measurements from the actual JP's Space Cadet v4.3 VPX, including its launcher
> and rules, use [Space Cadet reference](../docs/space-cadet-reference.md). Its
> current ball-relative comparison replaces the historical numeric comparisons below.

> **Project decisions that override this research** (set by the project's owner, 2026-11-02):
> - **The mirror axis is the middle of the play area, left of the screen's centre, with no filler piece.** Where the text below recommends keeping `apronL` as a filler and keeping the old centre, follow this instead. The left wall to the lane divider gives x = 0.461, which agrees with the 0.459 measured on the test table.
> - **Mirror only the lower kit.** Above the slingshots, tables are meant to differ left to right.
> - **No sunken launcher lane.** It's a flat lane beside the board.
> - **One aluminum ramp per level for now.** Where the text below suggests two ramps or wire ramps, follow this instead: wire ramps were tried on level 1 and rejected.
> - **A small second level is on level 1.** The text below advises waiting; the owner chose to include it.


Build each table in two parts: a lower third mirrored about the flipper centre line, and an upper two-thirds that is free, laid out as a graph of shots rather than a set of targets. Scale clearance features by ball diameter and gross placement by table width, because this game's ball is 14% larger than a real one and its board is narrower in ball terms, while its length above the flippers is almost exactly real (35.7 ball diameters against about 36). The current base is close to real proportions, but it departs in three ways worth a deliberate decision: a much narrower flipper gap (1.17 balls against about 1.83 on the one table measured), inlane rails 4 degrees off the flipper's top edge, and lanes at 1.04 balls against about 1.2. Level 1 (`aurora`) should lose its captive ball, magnet and possibly its drop bank, and become five to seven obvious shots that each return to a flipper. Second levels should wait, and the first should be a small timed side game, not a full upper playfield. Do not load or convert `.vpx` files for shipping tables; take ideas and reference numbers only. Most hard numbers in this field are thin: designer opinions below are almost all second-hand, and many dimensions rest on one homebrew build or one test table.

## Scale by the ball, because the board is real length but narrow

Two conversions apply. Real width is 20.25 in, so W (a fraction of width) equals inches × 0.0494 ([Pinball Makers](https://pinballmakers.com/wiki/Construction) [read]). The ball is 1 1/16 in, confirmed by VPX's constant of 50 units to one ball (`reference/repos/vpx_lightmapper/addons/vpx_lightmapper/vlm_utils.py` [read]), which is **0.0525 W against this game's 0.060**.

Comparing in ball diameters changes the picture. Against a real table, the game's flippers look 21% long and its pivots look far too low. In balls, the flipper is only 6% long (2.5 against 2.35), and the field above the flippers matches: **35.7 balls against 36.1**. The game's lane-top height also matches at 24.5 against 24.2. What differs is width: **16.7 balls across against 19.05**. The pivots are low because the game omits the apron strip, which holds nothing playable.

So the rule is: a clearance feature (lane, gap, ramp mouth) is sized in balls; position on the board is sized in W; and when the two collide, width is what runs out. From the flipper centre line to the lane divider, the blank table has 8.29 balls and the base has 7.3. A ball-scaled copy of the real lower kit therefore does not fit, and something has to give. In the base, the give comes from lane widths, while the rails are nearly twice as thick as real ones (see the table). That thickness comes from the art direction ("nothing thin"), so it is a deliberate trade, but it is where the lanes' missing width went.

There are two ways to recover width. Thinning rails from 0.042 to 0.030 W frees about 0.012 W per rail, enough to bring lanes to about 1.14 balls. Shrinking the ball to radius 0.0275 gives 7.96 balls of half-width, but it changes feel across all 30 levels and reduces readability on a phone. I would thin rails first and leave the ball alone [inference].

## Mirror the lower kit about the flippers, and spend the leftover width on one side

The standard bottom is one flipper, slingshot, inlane and outlane each side ([Kineticist](https://www.kineticist.com/news/understanding-the-italian-bottom) [snippet]). Asymmetric bottoms exist (European Paragon's three flippers, Harlem Globetrotters' dual left flippers) but are exceptions, and Judge Dredd's claimed extra inlane could not be confirmed ([Kineticist](https://kineticist.com/post/understanding-the-italian-bottom) [snippet]). No source states a height at which symmetry stops, or why designers break it. That it stops above the slings is reasoning from layouts, not a designer's statement.

The mirror axis is the flipper centre line, not the table centre, because the lane takes width from one side ([CMU teaching design](https://courses.ideate.cmu.edu/16-223/f2018/text/flatpack/pinball.html) [snippet]). The notes estimated the line 0.046 W left of sheet centre, and the blank table has it 0.041 W left [measured], so these agree. In the blank table the inlane guides mirror cleanly about that line (0.086 on the left maps to 0.832, against a measured 0.830), and the leftover width falls into the left outlane, making it 0.086 against 0.064. The caveat is that a further wall I did not identify may narrow that left outlane. The base's choice, documented in `Art/SCENES.md`, is to fill the leftover with `apronL` and equalise the outlanes. That is a sound simplification and I would keep it.

**The inlane guide should line up with the flipper's top edge, not its centre line.** A forum rule and a technician thread say the inlane and flipper share an angle ([Pinside](https://pinside.com/pinball/forum/topic/setting-flipper-heightangle) [snippet]; [Tilt Forums](https://tiltforums.com/t/flipper-alignment/823) [read]). The blank table shows the exact form. A flipper tapering from radius 20.59 to 11.77 over 117.65 units has a top edge 4.3 degrees steeper than its centre line, so a −30.5 degree rest gives a top edge at 34.8 degrees, and the guide's upper run measures 34–35.5 degrees. The base uses one `slope` of 25 degrees for flipper centre line and rail alike, while its top edge is at about 29. By my arithmetic the rail's top line reaches the flipper about 0.003 W below the flipper's top tangent point, a tiny lip, where the blank table's line arrives about 0.004 W above it, a tiny drop. This is small, one table's worth of evidence, and should be tested in slow motion before anything changes. If it shows as a hop, split `slope` into a flipper angle and a guide angle about 4 degrees steeper.

**The flipper gap is the largest departure from real, and it may be a good one.** The notes derived a gap of about 0.111 W from an assumed 2.75 in reach, with no tip radii. The blank table's true values give 0.096 W, or 1.83 balls. The base gives 0.070 W, or 1.17 balls: 25 degrees is flatter, and the flipper is proportionally longer. Players report that real Williams-style gaps feel wider than Zen's digital tables ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2328760/discussions/0/5276582532037113495) [snippet, anecdotal]), which suggests that a narrow gap is a known digital convention rather than a mistake. I would keep a narrow gap for level 1 and test a wider one for harder levels by setting rest to −30 degrees, which gives about 1.37 balls. Be aware that the shared base means any change here reaches all 30 tables and requires a retune.

Two smaller sourced rules fit here. A narrower outlane means fewer drains, and real machines offer post positions for this ([Flippers.be](https://www.flippers.be/basics/101_adjust_pinball.html) [read]), so outlane post position is a natural per-level difficulty dial, though it currently sits in the base. And safety features are legitimately one-sided: Indiana Jones's Narrow Escape is on the right outlane only ([IJ guide](https://www.cferrero.net/pball/IndianaJones.pdf) [read]), which the base already echoes with `kickbackL`.

## Build the launcher as a flat lane with a divider, not a trench

The sourced facts are that a wooden rail about 1/2 in wide separates lane from playfield ([Pinball Projects](https://pinballprojects.wordpress.com/2014/08/15/playfield-side-rails-and-ball-guides/) [read]; [VPForums](https://www.vpforums.org/index.php?showtopic=43101) [snippet]), and that width is 1 3/8 in on a Bally and 35 mm on a Firepower ([Pinside](https://pinside.com/pinball/forum/topic/general-lane-widths-and-flipper-spacing) [snippet]), which is 0.068 W or 1.30 balls. The blank table's divider is 0.024 W thick, matching that rail, and it runs up to **1.650 W, or 24.2 balls above the pivots** [measured]. The base's divider ends at 1.618 W, or 24.5 balls above them, so its length is right.

Width is less settled. The blank table's stop block ends at 0.973, which implies a lane of about 0.055 W (1.05 balls), but I inferred the right-hand face from that block alone. The base's clear width is 0.0655 W (1.09 balls). That is within the plausible range and I would not change it. The notes' suggestion of 0.078 W describes clear width, and the base's 0.078 is measured between wall centres, so do not read the two as already equal.

**Whether a real lane sits lower than the playfield is unconfirmed, and no source says it does.** A trough patent has the ball rise through the sheet and deflect horizontally into the lane, which places the lane at or above the surface in that design ([US5358243](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5358243) [read]). A homebrew uses only a local groove at the rod ([3D Wingmaster](http://3dwingmaster.blogspot.com/2015/07/the-shooter-lane.html) [read]). Zaccaria machines reportedly raised the lane ([Aussie Arcade](https://www.aussiearcade.com/topic/87851-shooter-lane-why-bother/) [snippet]). So keep the lane floor in the playfield plane, as now, and do not model a recess as if it were established. Settling this takes a straight edge across a real divider rail.

Above the divider, the blank table has a thin curved guide continuing from about (0.91, 1.65) up and left to about (0.795, 2.04) [measured; I identified its purpose by shape, its name is unclear]. If that reading is right, it carries the plunged ball into the top arc and forms the inner wall of the right orbit, so the lane and orbit are designed together. The one-way gate at the lane top is from general knowledge and no source read described its position, although the base's gate is the conventional shape. Rod height and ball-rest position were not found.

For a skill shot, pull distance sets launch force ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinball) [read]). On Lord of the Rings a strong plunge reaches the left flipper for a right-orbit skill shot ([LOTR guide](https://www.cferrero.net/pball/LoTR.pdf) [read]), showing a skill shot need not be a lane at the top. Drain-to-relaunch needs no visible track: the real trough is under the board ([US5497989](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5497989) [read]), so a timed teleport is faithful [inference].

## Lay out shots as a graph: flipper, shot, return, next flipper

Flow is contested, not universal. A podcast treats Ritchie's flow and Lawlor's stop-and-go as opposite poles ([Wedgehead](https://www.kineticist.com/promoters/wedgehead-pinball-podcast/episode-123-the-physical-game) [snippet]), and [Pinball Makers](https://pinballmakers.com/wiki/index.php?title=Design) [read] says there are no mandatory rules. The only first-hand-readable rule set is the Mission Pinball Framework guide, not attributed to any named designer, and it gives the two hard numbers in this whole topic: **fan layouts commonly have 7 or 8 shots, and each shot line from flipper to target needs half a ball of clearance** either side. It also says every shot should be makeable from at least one flipper, orbit guides should send the ball toward the flipper rather than a slingshot tip, steel near bumpers kills the action, and every switch needs feedback ([MPF](https://missionpinball.org/latest/physical_building/layout_considerations/) [read]).

The return paths on reference machines are well documented. On LOTR the left ramp feeds the left inlane, the Sword ramp feeds the right inlane, and right-orbit shots loop around the left orbit back to the flippers ([rules](https://pinballnirvana.com/forums/resources/lord-of-the-rings-stern-2003-vp8.2994) [read]). Indiana Jones's left ramp returns to the left flipper and its left orbit crosses to the right flipper or stops at rollovers ([guide](https://www.cferrero.net/pball/IndianaJones.pdf) [read]). The useful reading [inference] is that a ramp hands the ball to its own side and an orbit hands it across, so each return path is a commitment about which flipper plays next. That graph is the design object. KISS's 15 City Combos, such as Center Ramp to Right Ramp, read like paths through such a graph ([rulesheet](https://pinballrulesheets.com/stern/kiss-wiki-rulesheet.html) [read]).

Graded failure is the second sourced idea. LOTR's Ring ramp gives strong shots the Ring and sends weak ones into Barad-Dur or the O-R-C lanes, and its orbits behave similarly ([rules](https://pinballnirvana.com/forums/resources/lord-of-the-rings-stern-2003-vp8.2994) [read]). Medieval Madness's moat rolls weak shots back to the left flipper, and a patented deflector stops failed ramp shots draining down the middle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Madness) [read]).

The working procedure is a synthesis [inference], using the MPF rules above. Freeze the lower kit, which the shared base already does. Place the one spectacular feature and decide which side it takes from. Draw two fans of straight lines widened by half a ball, and check each flipper has an easy shot, a cross-shot and a risky one. Assign every shot three outcomes: made, weak and brick (full-speed rejection). Then write the graph and the combos it makes natural, spend the remaining space on nest and standups, and play it undecorated before art.

Second-hand designer views support this ordering but should not be quoted as fact. AI digests report that Keith Elwin starts from the "crazy" shots, that John Borg builds around a centrepiece, and that George Gomez holds that an undecorated whitewood must already be fun ([Elwin](https://www.kineticist.com/people/keith-elwin), [Borg](https://www.kineticist.com/people/john-borg), [Gomez](https://www.kineticist.com/people/george-gomez) [AI digest]). None was verified against a transcript. Ritchie's reported "sequentially fun" and billiards analogies also come only from digests, with no first-person text found.

Where the shots go wrong has less evidence. **No published method for preventing straight-down-the-middle rebounds was found.** Trade-offs are real: Twilight Zone's rubber bar blocked direct jets-to-outlane balls but encouraged centre drains, and later machines dropped it ([rulesheet](https://www.bmigaming.com/Games/Rulesheets/twilight_zone.txt) [snippet]). Forum players call saucers that fire down the middle a ripoff, and report that Stern adds brief ball saves after ejects ([Tilt Forums](https://tiltforums.com/t/making-a-successful-shot/1939) [read]). Since `make playtest`'s autopilot does not aim, a real test needs adding [inference]: fire each shot at full speed with small angle errors, and flag any rebound that reaches the flipper gap or an outlane mouth without touching rubber first.

Asymmetry above the slings comes from patterns observed in layouts; no source gives designer intent, so all of these are inferred. One big feature takes a side, as Indiana Jones's mini-playfield does on the upper left ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_Jones:_The_Pinball_Adventure) [read]). Bumpers go in one back corner: LOTR upper right, TOTAN back right ([rules](https://pinballnirvana.com/forums/resources/lord-of-the-rings-stern-2003-vp8.2994) [read]; [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tales_of_the_Arabian_Nights_(pinball)) [read]). Indiana Jones instead puts three pops just below central rollovers, so a centred nest is also legitimate. Returns stay mirrored while targets differ, and one ramp with several exits (TOTAN's, [Kineticist](https://www.kineticist.com/news/tales-of-the-arabian-nights-tutorial) [snippet]) adds variety without using width. This conflicts with the "keep the two sides the same" rule in `Art/SCENES.md`. That rule is right for the lower kit, but the research says relax `MIRRORED` above the slings. Otherwise each level is one layout with different props.

## Rebuild level 1 as five to seven shots that all come home

Evidence on beginners is community opinion only, with no player study and no designer statement. Attack from Mars is the most often cited beginner model: a fan of shots reachable from the two flippers, with a central toy, two ramps and two loops, and a centre saucer that returns the ball toward the flipper ([Pinball Mag](https://pinballmag.fr/en/attack-from-mars-pinball-machine-bally-retro-review/) [snippet]; [Kineticist](https://www.kineticist.com/post/attack-from-mars-pinball-tutorial) [snippet]). One caution from the notes: Lawlor and Elwin should not be cited as fan-layout models. Digests report that Elwin's Iron Maiden departs from the fan and that he finds basic fans too easy, and Lawlor is associated with crowded playfields ([Elwin](https://www.kineticist.com/people/keith-elwin) [AI digest]; [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pat_Lawlor) [snippet]). A search excerpt does have Lawlor saying middle shots are easier for beginners ([Gamasutra](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/interview-lawlor-on-modern-pinball-market-small-team-creativity) [snippet]), and a podcast page argues a centre ramp serves novices as a bailout ([Wedgehead](https://www.kineticist.com/promoters/wedgehead-pinball-podcast/episode-108-center-ramps) [snippet]). Those support a wide central shot.

`Scenes/tables/aurora.xml` carries more than this: mismatched `shortL longR` kits, three centred bumpers, a scoop, a captive ball, a drop bank that lights a lock, four stand-ups, and a magnet. By the staging above, the captive ball and magnet are mid- to late-game lessons. The proposal below is my design [inference], not a sourced recipe, and none of it has been playtested.

| Shot | Fed from | Made | Weak | Brick |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Left orbit | Right flipper | Crosses top, off toward right flipper | Falls into the rollovers or nest | Rubber-faced entry, deflects to a sling |
| Right orbit | Left flipper | Crosses top, off toward left flipper | Falls into the rollovers or nest | As above |
| Left ramp | Right flipper, or left backhand | Left inlane, left flipper | Rolls back, off to a flipper | Rubber sides, no direct line to the gap |
| Right ramp | Left flipper | Right inlane, right flipper | As above | As above |
| Centre scoop or wide target | Either flipper | Awards, then ejects to a flipper | Rebounds to a sling | Wide, so a brick is hard to produce |

Use a matching pair of ramp kits, as `Art/SCENES.md` advises. Keep the three-lane top and move the nest into one upper corner, with a standup pair or scoop on the opposite side as the mild asymmetry. That needs an exemption from the "bumpers on x = 0.461" rule. A centred nest remains acceptable if you prefer to spend the asymmetry elsewhere. Add one obvious goal: hit each of four shots three times. Space Cadet's repeat-the-element missions are a documented analogue ([StrategyWiki](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/3D_Pinball_for_Windows:_Space_Cadet/Commander_and_Commodore) [snippet]). Give each shot its own lamp, per MPF. Space Cadet's team avoided on-table text entirely for localisation, relying on visual cues ([developer account](https://ryanmedeiros.substack.com/p/space-cadet-3d-pinball-the-making) [read]). The base's 12-second ball save and left kickback stay.

One thing to check in the current table: the scoop at (0.38, 0.85) ejects along (−0.1, −1.0). As a straight line, that clears the gap, which is good, but by my arithmetic it passes within a ball radius of the left slingshot's lower third around y ≈ 0.32, and MPF specifically warns against balls striking sling tips. Gravity and speed will shift the real path, so replay it before trusting either figure.

Leave out upper flippers (MPF says their shots are harder and must have a feed; a digest reports Gomez calling them advanced), diverters, state-change inlanes, mini-playfields, and any eject down the middle. Then add one thing per level [inference]: drop bank and virtual lock, an off-centre feature, a variable-exit ramp, a graded-failure orbit, and a state-change inlane, as on LOTR where the left orbit from a right-inlane feed diverts to Orthanc ([rules](https://pinballnirvana.com/forums/resources/lord-of-the-rings-stern-2003-vp8.2994) [read]). Because autopilot does not aim, shot difficulty needs human playtests.

## Make second levels small, timed and at the back or side

Of the machines read, most "second levels" are not second playfields. Attack from Mars's saucer and Medieval Madness's castle are raised toys with no second ball surface, yet they deliver the sense of a raised centrepiece [read: [AFM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_from_Mars), [MM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Madness)]. The actual types are a full upper playfield reached by ramps with its own flippers (Black Knight, where the upper level cannot drain, so the lower was made fast and drain-prone to balance play time), a timed magnet field at the left edge with a success hole and a failure hole (Twilight Zone), a long tilting field at the left side (Indiana Jones), and an under-playfield basement (Theatre of Magic) [read: [BK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Knight_(pinball)), [TZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twilight_Zone_(pinball)), [IJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_Jones:_The_Pinball_Adventure), [ToM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Magic)]. A bi-level patent gives construction: boards gapped "slightly greater than" one ball, ramps with walls, upper flippers guarding the ramp top, and a hole as a way down, with an option for a transparent upper board ([US4606545](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4606545) [read]; which machine it covers is unconfirmed).

**Almost nothing published addresses readability from a shallow or portrait camera.** No developer statement from Zen or anyone else was found. The geometry is straightforward, though: a solid object of height h hides a floor strip of h / tan(e), where e is camera elevation. At 60 degrees elevation, assuming that is what the project's "30 degree camera" means, the strip is 0.58 h; at 30 degrees it is 1.73 h [estimate]. Perspective worsens this at the top of a portrait screen, which is exactly where upper levels traditionally sit. My ranking, unplaytested [inference], best to worst: wire ramps; toys with no ball surface; a side mini-playfield no wider than a quarter of the table; a low back platform with open sides or a translucent floor; an under-playfield, which hides nothing but loses the ball; and a full upper playfield. Keep anything tall out of the lower third, because flipper timing needs to see the ball arrive. Scale the ball with height as an "upstairs" cue, and draw a silhouette when it passes under a solid part. A camera push has a commercial precedent in Halloween Pinball's moving portrait camera ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/halloween-pinball/id719739502?ls=1) [read]). Judge all of this on the actual board render.

The engine path is small, per the notes' code reading and unbuilt design [inference]. Ball height is a mask (`PBLayerMask` in `App/Core/PBTableDefinition.h`), matching the pattern a Phaser/Box2D developer describes ([html5gamedevs](https://www.html5gamedevs.com/topic/4969-collision-on-different-heights-z-depth-in-a-top-down-view-game-pinball/?hl=pinball) [read]). Ramp completion currently sets `PBLayerPlayfield` unconditionally (`PBPhysicsWorld.m:967-985`), and a `ramp->exitLayer` is the smallest change that makes an upper level possible. The ball-to-ball check at line 996 should compare layers, not "on a ramp or not". A subway or VUK is a hidden timed state (`PBBallInTransit`, with a destination and a queue), matching VPX community practice of destroying and recreating balls ([Pinball Nirvana](https://pinballnirvana.com/forums/threads/shivaengines-features.12101/) [snippet]). A mini-playfield is a layer with local gravity, where Indiana Jones's tilt is gravity change and Twilight Zone's Magna-Flips reuse existing magnets. VP community clearance figures are 1.2 balls for a ramp's low end and 2.4 for a crossing ramp (same thread, unknown age). End a hidden transit within about a second or show an indicator, and extend ball search to new layers [estimate]. Keep a failure exit: every second level in the table has one, and it should return the ball to a flipper quickly.

## Do not use .vpx: the format is easy, but the content does not transfer

**Recommendation: keep your own XML, take ideas, and do not ship anything derived from a `.vpx`.** The container is not the obstacle. It is an OLE file of tagged records with one stream per element, readable in about 60 lines (`reference/repos/vpx_lightmapper/addons/vpx_lightmapper/biff_io.py` [read]), and MIT-licensed parsers exist ([vpin](https://github.com/francisdb/vpin) [read]). Nor is the coordinate conversion: `x' = x/952`, `y' = (2162 − y)/952`. A basic importer is estimated at 400–700 lines and 1–3 days [estimate].

Three things block it. First, **rules do not exist as data**: they are VBScript, often driving a ROM through PinMAME, and a ROM has no script to read ([Mobile.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vpinball/vpinball/master/docs/Mobile.md) [read]). A converted table is geometry with no game. Second, **the structure does not fit**: the shared base forbids tables from changing base parts ([Art/SCENES.md](SCENES.md)), real tables fail `MIRRORED`, and the example table's 18 ramp items include only 8 collidable ones, among them apron cards and invisible guides, so a person must decide which are shots [measured]. Collision can live in meshes, and raised walls and crossing ramps have no equivalent in the layer model. Third, **the rights belong to others**: community rules require the author's permission and treat silence as no ([VPUniverse](https://vpuniverse.com/forums/topic/7395-submissions-to-vpuniverse-modifications-rules-for-uploading-to-vpuniverse/) [snippet]), an author cannot grant a manufacturer's marks or art, and Pinball Arcade lost 61 of 94 tables when its Williams/Bally licence lapsed ([Pocket Gamer](https://www.pocketgamer.com/pinball-arcade/pinball-arcade-is-losing-61-of-its-tables-due-to-licensing-problems/) [read]). VPX's engine carries a clause forbidding use in a commercial product on part of its code ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vpinball/vpinball/master/LICENSE) [read]), and running script from imported files raises App Store guideline 2.5.2, with the free official app a precedent but not a guarantee ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/) [read]).

Nothing here is a legal conclusion. Williams v. Bally (1983), known only through a secondary summary, found game concepts and common pinball elements unprotected while audiovisual components could be ([Patent Arcade](https://www.patentarcade.com/?p=513) [read]). Whether copied layout geometry or trade dress is a risk, and whether a measuring script is fine, are questions for a lawyer. Hand-drawn original layouts without manufacturer names or art remain the clean position.

Worth taking, in priority order [inference]: per-element elasticity, friction and scatter, since scatter is a cheap way to stop repeating shots and yours are global constants; per-flipper strength, return speed and ramp-up, useful if a third flipper should feel different; hit thresholds on elements; and a private measuring script of about 150 lines that prints lane widths and part sizes and outputs nothing that ships. `docs/JP's VPX Physics 2026.pdf` in the VPX repo looks worth reading for tuning, though it was not read. Do not copy from `vpx_lightmapper`, which is GPL-3.0 (`reference/repos/vpx_lightmapper/LICENSE`), and resolve vpxtool's Apache versus MIT mismatch before bundling any of it. The one thing that would change this recommendation is an original table whose author gives written commercial permission, and even then a redraw in your XML would likely be cheaper.

## Conclusion

The useful surprise is that the game is less unrealistic than its raw numbers suggest. Measured in balls, its length, flipper size, lane height and sling height are near-real, and the actual distortions are narrow width and everything forced by it. This turns "should we match real dimensions?" into a narrower question: which width-driven compromises do you want? The gap and lanes are the two that shape play, and both are cheap to test on one branch before touching the 30 levels. A real whitewood measurement would be worth more than further web research, since it would settle the rest angle, tip gap, inlane and sling sizes, and lane recess with a single afternoon and a ruler, replacing a test table of unknown provenance.

The larger change is to the design rules rather than the geometry. The project's strongest rule, keep the two sides the same, is backed by research only for the bottom third. Applied above it, that rule prevents the thing reference tables use to give each layout a character. Limiting `MIRRORED` to the lower kit, adding an aimed-shot test that records where bricks land, and writing each level as a shot graph before placing parts would do more for the next 30 levels than any new mechanism, and all three cost less than a subway.
